Gravity and the Geometry of Motion
==================================

Introduction
------------

Two centuries after Newton devised his wildly successful interlocking theories of motion and gravity, Einstein realized there was a radically different way to think about them.  Instead of Newton's inverse square force of attraction between masses, Einstein proposed that mass bends time to produce the acceleration of gravity.  While Newton treats gravity like other forces, Einstein treats gravity differently.  Other forces extend through space and time, while gravity warps the connection between time and space.

Specifically, Einstein focuses on the fact that gravity produces identical acceleration for every body, independent of its size or composition, as Galileo taught before Newton.  In Newton's view, it could have been otherwise - gravity could have accelerated wood at a different rate than stone or gold.  Newton performed careful experiments with pendulums to show that the downward acceleration of gravity is, in fact, identical among different materials to within a tenth of a percent - three decimal places.  This independence of gravitational acceleration from material composition has now been confirmed to better than fifteen decimal places using test masses inside a specially designed satellite orbiting the Earth.  Einstein embraces these experimental results by postulating that the acceleration of gravity is a built-in property of the geometry of space and time (its gradient turns out to be the intrinsic curvature of spacetime) rather than of any material body.

Our goal here is to explore this question: How can Einstein's bizarre explanation of gravity as curvature be equivalent to Newton's common sense explanation of gravity as force?  Before we get started, you must be warned that our discussion will not follow the historical order or context in which Einstein developed his theories of Special Relativity and General Relativity.  In fact, Cartan developed the curvature interpretation of Newtonian gravity we present here, by back-engineering Einstein's General Relativity to the low speed limit (e.g.- the speed of anything in the solar system) where Newton's laws of motion and gravity are very nearly exact.  To understand the full theory of General Relativity, starting from Cartan's version of Newton's theory is easier than jumping directly from Newton to Einstein.  Here we focus on the radical change in viewpoint from Newton's picture of gravity to Einstein's, rather than on the details of the treatment of absurdly high speed motion.  In this low speed limit, the two theories really are exactly the same - Einstein just shows you can interpret Newton's laws very differently from how Newton intended, and still be consistent with the centuries of evidence supporting Newton's interpretation.

We open with a brief review of Newton's laws of motion, followed by a longer review Kepler's laws of planetary motion and Newton's theory of universal gravitation.  We continue with a discussion of Newton's concept of space and time, in which space is like the frames of a movie and time labels the frames.  World-lines of bodies tie the frames together, but the First Law of motion singles out a particular class of possible frame stackings for which the laws of motion apply.  Next, we contrast Newton's global with Einstein's local interpretation of the First Law.  Both use the First Law as a straightedge for spacetime, but they disagree about which world-lines are straight in the case of gravity.  This tension leads Einstein to the strange concept of an intrinsically curved spacetime in which the connection between movie frames varies from place to place.  We close with a discussion of the local reformulation of Newton's inverse square law and the matching local formulation of the laws of motion as the equations of fluid or solid mechanics, which Einstein needs to complete his local theory of gravity.


Newton's laws of motion
-----------------------

Newton's masterpiece, *The Mathematical Principles of Physics* - *Principia* for short - begins with a Definitions section which includes a lengthy discussion of how he thinks about time and space.  In particular, Newton introduces or perhaps justifies his picture of absolute time and space, to which we shall return.  Immediately after these definitions, he presents his three laws of motion.  Paraphrased, they are:

First Law: A body at rest stays at rest, and a body in motion stays in motion in a straight line at constant speed, unless some net force acts upon it.

Second Law: The net force on a body is proportional to its acceleration and to its mass.

Third Law: To every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.  That is, if body A exerts a force on body B, then B exerts an equal and opposite force on A.

The Second Law is Newton's iconic formula F=ma, which serves as a definition of force or mass, or even, as we shall see, acceleration - at any rate a relation among the three things.

The Third Law is now called "conservation of momentum," which is at first glance the only actual assertion being made about a feature of the physical world - you can't push on something without it pushing back on you.

An illustration from Galileo's masterwork "Dialogues Concerning Two New Sciences" shows a weight suspended by a rope from a cantilevered beam.  The picture suggests a way to think about force as tension in a rope which transmits force from one end to the other.

<img align="left" src="./galileo-fig.png" alt="Illustration from Dialogs" style="max-width: 50%; padding-right: 10px;"></img>

The tension will always produce equal and opposite forces at the two ends of the rope.  (Even when one side is winning a tug of war, the forces on the hands of the two teams are equal and opposite - it is the forces they are able to produce against the ground which differ.)  This is undoubtedly one of the pictures Newton had in mind when he formulated the Third Law: Imagine that any force between two bodies is transmitted by the tension of a thread (or compression of a rod) connecting them.  Much later, Faraday invented a similar picture of electric and magnetic field lines, which Maxwell showed act as innumerable tensioned threads connecting any two poles.  There is deep physics in the Third Law - it is a model for how bodies in the real world behave; it tells you the sense in which motion cannot be created or destroyed.

You may be puzzled by the First Law because it seems to be simply a special case of the Second Law, namely the case that the force and acceleration are both zero.  (Moving in a straight line at a constant speed, or not moving at all, is precisely what zero acceleration means.)  However, without the First Law, you don't really know how to measure acceleration - acceleration with respect to what?  You need some kind of a reference body you can take to be "at rest" - something relative to which you can measure position, hence velocity and acceleration.  "Relative to what?" is the question Newton is addressing with his lengthy discussion of absolute space preceding the laws of motion, and the purpose of the First Law is to answer that question.

The First Law tells you how to choose a reference body: Pick any body that has no net force acting on it - a free body.  If you have chosen your reference body according to the First Law, the Second Law tells your that any other free body will have zero acceleration relative to your reference.  That is, all free bodies move in straight lines at constant speed relative to one another, so no matter which free body you choose as your reference, all accelerations relative to it will be the same.  Thus, the physical content of the First Law for Newton is that acceleration is absolute - independent of whatever free body you choose as your reference for positions (and velocities).  But Einstein finds a loophole in the First Law, as we shall see.


Newton's universal gravitation
------------------------------

### Kepler's laws

Kepler's three laws of planetary motion distill millennia of astronomical observations, improving upon the already quite accurate ancient Greek system of epicycles described in Ptolemy's Almagest.  But unlike anyone before him, Kepler does not guess the distances to the planets; he surveys them, using pure geometry and a brilliant trick involving the periodicity of planetary orbits.  Working from naked eye observations before the invention of the telescope, Kepler discovered: (1) Planetary orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus and a fixed orientation with respect to the distant stars, (2) A radius drawn from the Sun to the planet sweeps over equal areas in equal times (so they move faster when closer to the Sun and slower when farther away), and (3) Comparing the orbits of the several planets, the cube of the major axis of their ellipse is proportional to the square of their orbital period.

Newton wrote Principia about three quarters of a century after Kepler.  Galileo had invented the telescope at essentially the same time Kepler was publishing his laws, and by Newton's time, astronomers knew that four moons of Jupiter and five moons of Saturn also obey Kepler's laws with respect to their planet; Kepler's laws of planetary motion had become Kepler's laws of orbital motion more generally.  (The constant of proportionality in the cube-square law is different for the Sun, Jupiter, and Saturn, however.)  After formulating the Second Law, F=ma, Newton would naturally ask what sort of force must be acting on the planets in order to keep them in their orbits?

### Centripetal forces and Keplerian orbits

To keep an object in circular motion (though ellipses, planetary orbits are very nearly circular) obviously requires a central force - a force directed toward the fixed point at the center of the orbit.  Just think about tying a stone to a string and whirling it in a circle.  The string is obviously pulling the stone directly toward the center - recall the purpose of the rope in Galileo's figure.  Hence it is not surprising that after stating his laws of motion, Newton turns his attention to how a body moves when the force on it is always directed toward toward some fixed central point - a centripetal force.  You can get non-circular orbits if you vary the tension in the notional string connecting the center point to the body by pulling in or letting out more string as it goes around, so the magnitude of the central force may vary, but the direction of the force is assumed to be toward a fixed center.

Newton's very first result is that for any centripetal force, the radius drawn from the center point to the body sweeps out equal areas in equal times, and the motion remains in a single plane containing the center point.  Thus, Kepler's second law of planetary motion is an immediate consequence of F=ma, assuming the force holding the planet in its orbit is always directed toward a fixed point.  Newton also proves the converse: If you observe any body to move such that its radius to some fixed point sweeps out equal areas in equal times, then the body must be experiencing a force always directed toward that fixed point.  Right out of the gate, Newton has shown that the planets experience a force always directed toward the Sun!  Furthermore, the moons of Jupiter always accelerate directly toward Jupiter, and the moons of Saturn directly toward Saturn!  Today, Kepler's equal area law is called "conservation of angular momentum"; again, it is a consequence of F=ma for any centripetal force.

Because of Kepler's equal area law, all possible motion under any centripetal force must happen in a single plane containing the center of force.  Furthermore, since the area swept out is proportional to time, area in the plane of motion can be taken as a surrogate for time, and the entire problem reduces to a study of curves in plane geometry.  Newton is very, very good at geometry, and he proceeds to work out exactly how the force (or rather acceleration, since the mass of the body is fixed) must vary from place to place in order for the trajectory of the body to follow half a dozen simple plane figures.

Needless to say, one of the figures he chooses is an ellipse from Kepler's first law of planetary motion.  That is, Newton asks, "How must the centripetal force on a body vary in order for its trajectory to be an ellipse with the center of force at one focus?"  Working through the geometry, he finds that the force must vary inversely as the square of the distance from the center.  He finds the same inverse square law is required for the body to follow a hyperbolic or parabolic trajectory with the center at one focus - any conic section.  (The hyperbola is interesting because one of its two branches is the case of a repulsive inverse square force; the other conic orbits all require attractive inverse square forces.)

Some other figures Newton works out are an elliptical trajectory with force directed to the center of the ellipse instead of to a focus, an Archimedean spiral trajectory with center at its endpoint, and a circular trajectory with the center on the perimeter of the circle.  These require, respectively, force directly proportional to distance, force inversely proportional to the cube of distance, and force inversely proportional to the fifth power of distance.  He's having so much fun you can feel his joy through all the formality of the translated Latin text.

Now that Newton has shown an inverse square law of centripetal force is mathematically equivalent to Kepler's first two laws of planetary motion, all that remains is Kepler's cube-square law relating the orbits of multiple satellites around a common center.  For the case of circular orbits, the cube-square law is obvious, inasmuch as acceleration scales as distance divided by the square of orbital period, and itself scales inversely with distance squared.  In fact, the constant of proportionality in the cube-square law is the same as the constant of proportionality in the inverse square acceleration law, to within some factors of two pi.  To wrap up the case, Newton compares circular and elliptical orbits sharing a common inverse square acceleration law.  He shows that their orbital periods match when the major axis of the ellipse equals the diameter of the circle (which is also its major axis).

Hence, for any orbital shape, Kepler's third law means that all the planets (or moons) accelerate toward the Sun (or planet) according to a single inverse square acceleration law.  If you know how far anything is from the Sun, you know how fast it is accelerating toward the Sun.  Similarly, you know how fast any body accelerates toward Jupiter from its distance to Jupiter or toward Saturn from its distance to Saturn.

### Eureka! Universal gravitation

To put a cherry on top, Newton points out that the Moon is 60 Earth radii from the center of the Earth, and the fact that the Moon takes a bit more than 27 days to make each orbit (with respect to the stars) means that its acceleration toward the Earth is 3600 times less than the acceleration of gravity on Earth's surface.  But 1/3600 is the inverse square of 60, so if the gravitational force we feel here diminishes as the inverse square of the distance from Earth's center, it is exactly the force required to hold the Moon in its orbit!  Newton has not simply shown that an inverse square acceleration law is mathematically equivalent to Kepler's three laws of planetary motion, he has demonstrated that this inverse square force is none other than the force of gravity we have always known.  Not only are the planets moving according to F=ma like any stone we throw, but also the force holding planets in orbit is the familiar pull of gravity.  This is pretty easily the greatest Eureka moment in the history of human thought - Newton has literally connected heaven to Earth.

<img align="right" src="./newton-fig.jpg" alt="Illustration from System of the World" style="max-width: 50%; padding-left: 10px;"></img>

Here is a figure Newton draws in *System of the World*, his informal explanation of Principia.  It shows that a cannonball fired from a high mountain with greater and greater muzzle velocity will eventually fall all the way around the Earth, remaining in orbit (assuming no air resistance), effectively becoming a tiny moon.  This close-in moon will be accelerating toward the center of the Earth at one-gee (like any cannonball), so you can easily work out the period of a moon in what is now called low Earth orbit (LEO).  You will find that this LEO moon and our natural Moon obey Kepler's cube-square law.  Again, the force of gravity any cannonball or thrown stone feels is exactly the force needed to hold the Moon in orbit, assuming gravity obeys the inverse square law.

But there's more, much more.  From the moons of Jupiter and Saturn as well as our Moon and Earth, in addition to the planets and Sun, Newton has demonstrated that the Sun, Jupiter, Saturn, and the Earth each attracts any body in its neighborhood with an acceleration that decreases inversely as the square of distance from it.  It's a small step to imagine this inverse square law of gravity is an innate property of all bodies, even if it is an imperceptibly small force between any pair of bodies much smaller than planets.  Hence, Newton postulates that his inverse square law is a universal force of gravitation acting between any pair of bodies whatsoever.

Furthermore, Newton has only used his Second Law of motion to prove all of this!  What does the Third Law tell him about his inverse square law of gravity?

Since gravity accelerates all bodies at the same rate - an experimental fact - the Second Law means force of gravity must be proportional to the mass of the body being pulled, be it a small weight on Earth's surface, a moon, or a planet.  By the Third Law, that smaller body must be exerting exactly the same force as the gravity of the larger body exerts upon it - you must be pulling upward on the Earth with exactly the same force the Earth pulls downward on you according to the Third Law of motion.  That counterforce can be none other than the gravitational attraction of your own body, since it must vary inversely as the square of the distance from you!  Hence the force of gravity must be proportional not only to the mass of the body being pulled, but also to the mass of the body doing the pulling - that's the only way the force and counterforce can be equal and satisfy the Third Law.  Not only is the gravitational force a body experiences proportional to its mass (acceleration is independent of composition or size), but the gravitational force it exerts on other bodies must also be proportional to its mass by the Third Law.  (Incidentally, the reasoning which led Einstein to the field equations of General Relativity is based on this intimate connection between gravity and conservation of momentum.)

Thus, the gravitational force between two bodies is proportional to the product of the two masses and inversely proportional to the square of their distance.  The constant of proportionality - Newton's gravitational constant - is the first universal constant ever discovered.  Newton computed its value when mass is measured in units of solar masses or Earth masses, but the experiment required to measure its value with mass in units of kilograms - measuring the gravitational attraction between two bodies with known masses in kilograms - is extraordinarily delicate, and wasn't successfully carried out until a century after Principia.  Newton's constant with mass in kilograms remains the least accurately known of all universal constants - astronomers often use solar masses instead of kilograms because Newton's constant is extremely accurately known only in those units of mass.


World-lines and the direction of time
-------------------------------------

### Movies, card decks, and world-lines

We very naturally think about motion as rapid succession of snapshots or frames, each capturing some group of objects at a single instant of time, because that is exactly how we make movies or videos or any animation.  When the snapshots are presented in rapid sequence, we interpret changes in position of the objects from frame to frame as motion.  Although the frames of a movie or video are two dimensional, it is easy to imagine a succession of three dimensional frames, each depicting some group of objects at a different instant of time.  Euclidean geometry governs each individual frame so we know all about straight lines, circles, distances, angles, and so on in each frozen snapshot - that's exactly what Euclid was trying to capture.  The problem comes when we try to connect the successive frames of our movie, and that connection is what Newton is addressing with his discussion of absolute time and space.

For two dimensions of space, you can imagine each snapshot is reproduced on a card, where each card represents one instant of time.  For example, imagine positions the Sun, Earth, and Mars (as viewed from far above the ecliptic plane) at successive times are marked on each card, so you can label each card by the time of its snapshot.  When we talk about spacetime, we are talking about the whole deck, stacked in order, and we are interested in the geometry of that whole stack.  But when you place each card atop the previous one, how do you know where to put it?  These cards have no edges to line up.  In fact, in the case of the solar system cards, Ptolemy used the Earth to align successive cards the deck, while Copernicus used the Sun - geocentric stacking versus heliocentric stacking.  In general, you are free to slide the cards around however you wish: What does it mean to be "at the same place" at two different times?  Relative to the Earth? to the Sun? or to something else entirely?

<img src="./fig_heliogeo.png" alt="Heliocentric and geocentric orbits" style="width: 90%; padding-left: 5%;"></img>

The three dashed triangles in this figure indicate the relative positions of the Sun (yellow), Earth (blue), and Mars (orange) at three different times.  The faded trails indicate the trajectories sampled by the dots.  On the left is the heliocentric stacking of the spatial slices, in which successive cards are positioned to put the Sun vertex at the same place at all times, as advocated by Copernicus and Kepler.  On the right is the geocentric stacking, in which the cards are positioned to keep the Earth vertex at the same place, as advocated by Ptolemy and the majority of ancient Greek astronomers.  With geocentric stacking, the trajectory of Mars (or any other planet) follows a complicated shape the Greeks modeled using epicycles.  In general, changing how you stack the cards warps the trajectories of the bodies you are tracking.

However you stack the spatial slices, when you connect the position of any material body on one slice to its position on successive slices, you get a (possibly curved) line which runs out of the spatial dimensions through the time dimension.  This is called the world-line of the body.  The following figure shows the world-lines of the Sun, Earth, and Mars over exactly the same time period as the previous figure.  Spacetime geometry is more about these world-lines than about the points (called events) along them.  When you use any particular body as your reference guide for stacking spatial slices, its world-line becomes a straight line which cuts through spacetime perpendicular to the deck in our card model, as does the world-line of any other body which is at rest relative to the first.  Bodies moving with constant velocity (speed and direction) relative to the reference body will also have straight world-lines, but tilted away from the perpendicular to the card deck.  The world-lines for other bodies will be distorted into various curves.  In the solar system example, every body except whatever you choose as a reference will corkscrew through the card deck.

<img src="./fig_wrldln.png" alt="Heliocentric and geocentric orbits" style="width: 90%; padding-left: 5%;"></img>

For Newton, the First Law determines which stacking should be used to decide on the straightness - that is, acceleration - of each of these world-lines.  The stacking he uses turns out to be almost but not quite the heliocentric stacking on the left, so that the world-line of the Sun is (nearly) straight, while the world-lines of the planets are curved into their corkscrew paths.  Einstein, on the other hand insists that the world-lines of the Sun and all the planets are straight in both the heliocentric and geocentric stacking, because they are all in free fall, and the First Law therefore requires they all experience zero acceleration (curvature).  Given these spacetime diagrams with their corkscrews, this looks crazy - how can he get away with such an assertion?

### The direction of time

<img align="left" src="./fig-tilt.svg" alt="Illustration from Dialogs" style="max-width: 50%; padding-right: 10px;"></img>

As you sit in your chair, you can easily point forward, backward, left, right, up, or down - corresponding to the three pairs of opposite directions in three dimensional space.  If you want to think about spacetime geometrically, then future and past must correspond to a fourth direction - your time direction.  Going back to the card deck analogy, the direction of time must punch through the deck, just like every world-line.  If you stack the card deck so that you and your chair are at rest, then your direction of time must be along your world-line (blue), perpendicular to the cards in the deck.  But perhaps you look out your window and see someone driving by in a car.  Their world-line (orange) will be tilted as it cuts through your card deck - thus, their direction of time is not the same as yours!  The direction of time varies from one observer to another, depending on how fast and in what direction they are moving.

This is no different from the fact that the spatial directions like forward or right depend on what direction you are facing.  The direction of time, like the direction rightward, is relative.  Velocity measures the tilt of world-lines, hence the tilt of the time direction relative to some particular card stacking, similar to the way that angle (or more closely, slope) measures the tilt of rightward for you relative to rightward for someone standing nearby.  If their rightward is different from yours, it means they are facing in a different direction.  If their time direction is different from yours, it means they are moving with a different velocity.  Spatial directions depend on orientation, the time direction depends on motion.

To summarize, the direction of time runs along world-lines, and motion tilts the direction of time in the same way that rotation tilts spatial directions.  But in addition to relative directions like rightward, we also have absolute directions like north (taking "north" in its astronomical sense to mean Polaris-ward).  Is there an absolute direction for time?  Newton says yes - that's what his discussion of absolute space and time is about.  However, he concedes that, like compass directions, there is some arbitrariness about exactly what choice you make for the standard direction of time.  There is no preferred state state of motion, any more than there is a preferred direction in space.

Newton suggests using the world-line of the center of mass of the entire solar system, which is called the solar system barycenter (SSB), as a practical realization of an absolute direction of time.  He proves that, according to the laws of motion, this point moves at a constant velocity relative to any distant body which has no forces acting upon it.

Note that the center of the Sun moves around the SSB as the configuration of the planets change due to their various orbits.  Thus Newton's solar system model is not (or only approximately) heliocentric, a fact generally overlooked.  Newton has rendered the revolutionary ideas of Copernicus moot (if not silly) less than a century and a half after that bitter controversy ignited.  The world-line of the SSB, always within about a solar diameter of the center of the Sun, is the practical absolute direction of time to this day, used by all modern astronomers.

Incidentally, the practical absolute direction in space, called the International Celestial Reference Frame or ICRF, is based on the directions to several hundred extremely distant quasars evenly scattered throughout the sky.  In Newton's day, the ultimate basis of absolute direction in space was distant stars.  Thus, the ideas behind the practical realization of absolute directions in spacetime generally have changed very little, although the details have been greatly refined.

### Timelike and spacelike directions

Notice that although the tilt of a world-line - the velocity of the body - is completely unrestricted, it cannot tilt backward in time.  The direction of time differs from any spatial direction in that particular, since you can reorient yourself in space to make any direction at all be rightward.  The reason is simple: you would never interpret a world-line to point backward in time - if the body were following such a world-line, you would interpret it as moving forward in time with the opposite velocity.  But even including both directions along it, no world-line can point directly along any spatial direction.  Again, the reason is simple: no body can be in two (or multiple) places at once.  Hence, there are technically two different kinds of directions in spacetime, called timelike directions - directions a world-line can lie along - and spacelike directions - the directions in ordinary space at any given instant of time.

Einstein was the first to realize that this structure is not quite correct when bodies move ridiculously fast, which he did a decade before his theory of gravity.  It turns out that the distinction between timelike and spacelike directions in spacetime is still important, but that the spacelike directions are not quite restricted to directions in ordinary space, since no world-line can tilt faster than light.  But we will continue to ignore that high speed detail of the story here - it is a plot complication that distracts from the more important new perspective Einstein brings to the story of gravity.


Using the First Law as a straightedge
-------------------------------------

### A straightedge for world-lines

So far, spacetime isn't much of a geometry.  A collection of world-lines seems more like a tangle of spaghetti; the shape of each strand depends entirely on how you choose to stack the spatial slices of spacetime - how you align successive cards in the deck.  The curviness of the world-lines depends on the stacking, so if you had a rule for determining which world-lines are "straight", it would greatly constrain how you could stack the cards.

In ordinary geometry, Euclid's first two postulates declare that straight lines exist, that is, that there is such a thing as a straightedge.  However, the practical embodiment of the postulates is left to the reader.  Newton writes very beautifully about the relation between this abstract geometry and practical mechanics in his preface to Principia:

<p style="margin-left: 2em;">
"To describe right lines and circles are problems, but not geometrical problems. The solution of these problems is required from mechanics; and by geometry the use of them, when so solved, is shown; and it is the glory of geometry that from those few principles, brought from without, it is able to produce so many things. Therefore geometry is founded in mechanical practice, and is nothing but that part of universal mechanics which accurately proposes and demonstrates the art of measuring."
</p>

With his laws of motion - he also calls them axioms - Newton clearly intends to extend these mechanical underpinnings of Euclidean geometry from position in space to motion in spacetime.  If we had a straightedge for spacetime, we'd have the beginnings of a real geometry of spacetime.

But the First Law is exactly the straightedge we need for world-lines!  If you can find any body which has no net forces acting upon it, the First Law says it is either at rest or moving with constant speed and direction - in either case, its world-line must be a straight line through spacetime.  This drastically narrows down the ways you are permitted to stack the spatial snapshots to build up spacetime, since every body experiencing no net force can at most be moving at a constant speed in a fixed direction relative to your reference body.  The remaining arbitrariness is just like Euclid's straightedges: You can tilt your spacetime straightedge to make it point in whatever time direction you choose.  That is, you can select a reference body moving with any velocity, as long as it is freely falling.

Now Newton presumes that any body very far from any other will have no significant external force acting upon it; by the First Law, such a remote body must have a straight world-line.  This single remote body may therefore serve as a reference, determining how to stack the spatial slices throughout all of spacetime.  He demonstrates that the center of mass of the entire solar system (the SSB) must move in a straight line through spacetime with this same stacking.  Thus, to the extent that the solar system is far from anything else, he doesn't need to rely on a hypothetical remote body to stack the spatial slices.  With this global stacking, the world-lines of the planets and satellites are all curved, corkscrew-like paths through spacetime.  Some force must cause those world-lines to curve, and we have seen how Newton uses his Second and Third Laws to work out universal gravitation.

### First Law redux

Despite the wild successes of Newtonian gravity, Einstein is uneasy with Newton's interpretation of the First Law: When you are falling, you feel no forces acting upon you - orbiting astronauts are weightless!  Why shouldn't the First Law require that you be moving in a straight line at a constant speed?  Any material body will develop internal stresses when a net external force is applied to its surface, like the rope in Galileo's illustration of a suspended weight.  Newton's answer to this criticism is that gravity acts directly on each small part of the material in a body, so unlike a rope that pulls only on its attachment point, gravity produces no differential forces inside a falling body and hence no internal stresses.

In a way, Newton's answer works a little too well - it applies not only to the individual parts of a single body which are at rest relative to one another, but also to any nearby bodies that may be falling at different instantaneous speeds - these also experience the same gravitational acceleration as the first body, so relative to that body, they continue moving in straight lines with fixed directions.  That is, any collection of freely falling bodies nearby that first body obey the First Law locally among themselves.  Einstein points out that locally this collection of nearby small objects would look exactly the same if the whole collection were removed to a remote location, but observed from an accelerated vantage point.  In other words, at least locally, the action of gravity is indistinguishable from adopting the viewpoint of an accelerated observer.

Interestingly, Newton actually calls attention to this feature of the relative motion of a collection of objects in Principia as well (Corollary VI after the Laws of Motion).  This concept is crucial for understanding why the system of moons around any planet orbit according to Kepler's laws at the same time that the planet itself orbits the Sun according to those same laws, which is why Newton takes care to point it out.

Einstein takes this principle to the extreme by proposing that instead of Newton's global stacking of spatial slices to build a complete model, we should allow ourselves to use many local stackings, and figure out how to piece them together into a complete whole.  Newton imagines a global connection between snapshots, while Einstein imagines many local connections, so different parts of each snapshot connect to corresponding parts of adjacent snapshots differently.  From a global perspective, this is obviously dramatically more complicated than Newton's picture.  Despite its complexity, Einstein's local interpretation of the First Law is arguably more practical - if a body has no internal stresses, you can use it as a reference body locally.  Weightlessness is the test for world-line straightness.  That is, any body in free fall is moving in a straight line through spacetime locally according to Einstein.

Einstein applies the First Law very differently from Newton; both recognize that it defines straightness of world-lines, but come to very different conclusions about which world-lines are straight.  For both Newton and Einstein, the SSB world-line is straight.  With his global reading of the First Law, Newton concludes that the planets accelerate toward the Sun and therefore have curved world-lines due to the force he identifies as gravity.  With his local reading of the First Law, Einstein concludes that any body in free fall experiences no net force and therefore is not accelerating; the planets and all their satellites have straight world-lines.  These different interpretations of the First Law - local versus global - lead to very different perspectives on the nature of gravity - force versus spacetime curvature.


Does the ground accelerate upward?
----------------------------------

### Bouncing balls

Let's look at a concrete example: Watch two balls bounce when dropped from different heights.  Physics lecturers often use a ball bearing dropped onto a heavy steel plate, which can bounce for several minutes, returning to nearly the same height on successive bounces - we're imagining two such ball-and-plates next to each other on the ground.  Except for the instants of contact, both balls are in free fall.  The motion is in one spatial dimension - vertical - so we can easily draw the world-lines of these balls in spacetime by plotting time horizontally.  Each of our one dimensional spatial snapshots records three vertical positions - the ground (where the plates they bounce on sit) and the two balls.

<img src="./fig_nbounce.png" alt="Newton bouncing balls spacetime diagram." style="width: 90%; padding-left: 5%;"></img>

Newton says that the force of gravity constantly accelerates each ball downward, so its trajectory on each bounce curves in a parabola through spacetime.  The ground, on the other hand, remains at a fixed position, held there by the gradually increasing pressure in deeper and deeper layers of rock under it, ultimately extending to the center of the Earth.  In order to judge that the balls are accelerating downward, Newton implicitly uses the ground (or the center of the Earth) as his reference body to stack the snapshots, so that he plots the ground as a straight horizontal world-line in our diagram.

Einstein says that since the balls are in weightless free fall between bounces, unstressed by any external forces, we should stack the snapshots so that every bounce of both is a straight world-line rather than a parabola.  The velocity or tilt of these world-lines of course changes at the moment of each impact, when the ball certainly does experience very large internal stresses and thus changes velocity very rapidly.  In any stacking with this property, the ground will follow an upward curving parabola, that is, the ground is accelerating upward at one gee in Einstein's picture of spacetime.

<img src="./fig_ebounce.png" alt="Einstein bouncing balls spacetime diagram." style="width: 90%; padding-left: 5%;"></img>

Notice that with a single stacking, Einstein turns every bounce of both balls into a straight segment of their world-lines, despite the fact that their relative velocities differ and change with each bounce.  This is because the acceleration of every ball in Newton's stacking was one gee downward, no matter what its instantaneous velocity.  Notice that Einstein has a very simple explanation for Galileo's observation that all objects accelerate downward at the same rate relative to the ground: It's the ground that accelerates upward at one gee - the acceleration doesn't belong to the freely falling objects at all! Their world-lines are all straight!  When Newton keeps the ground "at the same place" for all times, he has chosen an upwardly accelerating reference frame, so naturally anything he observes from that viewpoint will appear to share the opposite downward acceleration.

But what happened to gravity in Einstein's picture?  If there is no such thing as a force of gravity, what keeps the planets orbiting the Sun?  The problem is that Einstein's stacking of spatial slices only works as long as Newton's acceleration of gravity does not change from place to place.  The Earth is a globe, after all, and on the opposite side from where you sit, their down is your up - the acceleration of gravity is in completely the opposite direction.  Hence Einstein's stacking can only apply locally - he has to use a different stacking on the opposite side of the Earth.  Although the downward acceleration of gravity remains pretty nearly the same within a few kilometers of where you are sitting, it begins to change at distances which are a significant fraction of the size of the Earth.  So you can use Einstein's stacking within a few kilometers (and a few seconds) of where (and when) you are, but you cannot use it globally.

### Down the deepest hole

Imagine that the Earth weren't spinning, and that you drill a hole straight through its center and out the opposite side.  Newton's acceleration of gravity must decrease gradually as you move down the hole to zero at the center, then grow back to one gee in the opposite direction when you reach the other end of the hole.  Splitting the hole into a number of segments each a few kilometers long, Newton's acceleration of gravity changes very little within each segment, so you can apply Einstein's stacking to each segment individually.  This produces a whole family of parabolas, each a few kilometers in vertical extent but covering as great an extent of time as you please.  The curvature of the parabolas gradually decreases until you reach the central one, which is a straight horizontal line - Einstein's stacking and Newton's stacking agree at the center of the Earth.  Continuing down the hole, Einstein's parabolas gradually become more and more curved downwards.  This is what Einstein means by a local stacking of snapshots - each local stacking is almost but not quite consistent with adjacent stackings, causing the gaps between adjacent parabolas in this example.  (Continuing above the surface of the Earth the parabolas begin to overlap, so that's harder draw, but the idea is the same.)

<img src="./fig_deepest.png" alt="Interrupted deepest hole spacetime diagram." style="width: 90%; padding-left: 5%;"></img>

What happens when you drop a ball down the hole?  In each of the parabolic strips, we know that its world-line will be straight.  But what happens when its world-line reaches the edge of one of the gaps in our diagram?  Despite the gaps between parabolas, adjacent edges really represent the same curved world-line, just stacked slightly differently.  Where the straight world-line of the ball meets the curved world-line of the edge, there is a relative velocity between ball and edge, which is the difference in slope in our interrupted space-time diagram.  That difference in velocity or slope must be the same on the other side of the gap, which means the slope of the ball's world-line will be tilted toward Earth's center as it crosses each gap in our local stacking diagram.  Within each parabolic strip the ball moves in a straight line, but the slope of that line jumps as it crosses from one strip to the next, so by the time it reaches the central strip it is moving downward quite rapidly.  After crossing the center, it slows and will continue oscillating between the two ends of the hole indefinitely.

This picture is insanely complicated compared to Newton's picture of the same process - for Newton the ball experiences a downward force that decreases to zero at the center then reverses, so it naturally oscillates between opposite ends of the hole in the same way it would if attached to a spring, with a curved world-line.  Newton has no gaps to jump or differences in slope to keep track of - his global stacking has a dramatically simpler spacetime geometry than Einstein's local stacking.  But to close those gaps, Newton had to make the world line of the freely falling ball curved, that is, the ball has to be accelerating toward the center of the Earth.

<img src="./fig_newton.png" alt="Newton's deepest hole spacetime diagram." style="width: 90%; padding-left: 5%;"></img>

The advantage of Einstein's picture only shows up when the deck-of-cards structure of spacetime (really Newton's concept of absolute time) breaks down for extremely high speed motion, which we are not considering here.  For now, we note that within each parabolic strip of spacetime in the local stacking picture, we don't need to understand how gravity changes the motion of small bodies - they simply move in straight lines like free bodies; gravity only enters in how the local strips are connected.  Thus, if you have good local models of how all the forces besides gravity affect small bodies, you can use those models to figure out the motion of everything within each parabolic strip.  Einstein calls this the Equivalence Principle.  Gravity has been reduced to a purely geometrical problem; it changes the velocity of bodies only when they cross from one strip to the next.

(In the mathematical jargon of curved spacetime, Newton's three components of the acceleration of gravity are called connection coefficients, or even more frighteningly, Christoffel symbols.)

The time drawn at the apex of all the parabolas is completely arbitrary, so you can choose it to be whatever time you wish, without changing the relative positions of any bodies or world-lines within any strip.  One way to visualize how these strips fit together is to make a movie with the parabola apexes shifted to progressively later times.  Each movie frame shows the motion, that is  world-line, of everything at all times, so it isn't really time which is changing in the movie.  Instead, it's analogous to making a movie by panning over a single still frame.  By drawing a dot representing the dropped ball at the apex time, however, you can watch the ball oscillate between the ends of the hole, although its world-line remains straight within each strip.  The apparent curved motion comes from the changing tilt of the section of the world-line in each strip as its parabola apex shifts.

Intrinsic curvature of spacetime
--------------------------------

Although each of the parabolic strips appears curved in Einstein's interrupted spacetime diagram of a ball dropping down the deepest hole, that is only because the edges of the strips have been taken to be points at constant distance from the center of the Earth.  The whole idea is that Einstein considers any such point to be accelerating upward (apart from the point at the center).  Any body freely falling down the hole follows a perfectly straight line within every strip.  Thus, the curvature of the strips in this diagram is not what Einstein means by spacetime curvature - the curvature of the strips is merely the acceleration of local sections of the hole wall; it has nothing to do with the dropped ball.

Spacetime curvature, as Einstein wants you to picture it, is the fact that gaps (or overlaps in other cases) appear between the individual strips.  The width of these gaps is proportional not to the curvature of the individual strips, but to the slight change in curvature between each strip and its neighbors.  That is, the gaps depend not on the local acceleration of (Newtonian) gravity, but on its gradient, that is, on how rapidly the acceleration of gravity is changing from place to place.  Einstein's spacetime curvature is the gradient of Newton's gravitational acceleration.  In mathematical jargon, that gradient of acceleration is called the Riemann curvature tensor (technically, the non-zero components of that tensor).

This sort of compound curvature - the changes in curvature from one strip to the next in our example - is called intrinsic curvature.  Gauss identified and began studying intrinsic curvature of two dimensional surfaces, and left his student Riemann to generalize his work in several important respects, to both higher dimensions and a more general notion of distance.  Einstein could use Riemann's geometry as the mathematical framework for his physical picture of curved spacetime a century later.

Another simple special case is the two dimensional geometry of the surface of a sphere.  You are very familiar with this case from geography: Flat maps a few kilometers on a side are exceedingly useful for finding your way around locally, but as you try to piece them together to display larger and larger regions, you soon realize that they don't quite fit together perfectly, because the surface of the Earth is not flat.  If the Earth were cylindrical, your local maps would still fit together perfectly, because you know you can easily roll a flat sheet of paper into a cylinder without crinkling it.  The problem with the globe is that it curves in both directions, while the cylinder is only curved along one direction.  This is the compound or intrinsic curvature of a surface that Gauss studied.

A flat map of the entire globe always splits open, like when you try to flatten an orange peel.  The gaps that open when you try to flatten a world map are unavoidable - cartographers call them interruptions - if you want the map to be undistorted.  There are many ways to place the interruptions - some of the art of cartography is choosing interruptions to support the purpose of the map.  Here is a global map (based on the polyconic projection) with a rather ugly choice of interruptions, in which the interruptions follow lines of latitude, so the interruptions are in the lines of longitude:

<img src="./fig_polycone.png" alt="Interrupted polyconic world map." style="width: 90%; padding-left: 5%;"></img>

As you can see, this map of Earth's surface strongly resembles the Einstein's map of spacetime for our deepest hole example.  Time and vertical position are analogues of longitude and latitude, respectively.  Instead of parabolas, the world map strips are curved in circles, but apart from that the resemblance is striking.  Lines of latitude are curved toward the nearest pole in this flattened diagram, except for the equator, which is straight.  The black line is another straight line - a great circle running between 50 degrees north and south latitude, and it also appears straight in every strip.  In fact, if you start walking due east and keep walking in a straight line, your latitude inevitably bends toward the equator, and you end up following a great circle.  In the same way, if you begin at rest and advance in time along a straight world-line in Einstein's map, you inevitably accelerate toward the center of the Earth.

From Einstein's perspective, by leaving out the interruptions Newton has inevitably distorted spacetime, in the same way that you must inevitably distort a world map by drawing it on a flat surface without interruptions.  However, unlike the world map, you can argue about whether it is Newton's or Einstein's spacetime map which is distorted - the question boils down to whether you require a global inertial frame or permit local inertial frames where you will apply the First Law.  Everyone admits that absolute velocity is meaningless; the question is whether you you insist acceleration be absolute (Newton) or permit it to be relative to nearby bodies (Einstein).  Perhaps it is best to say that either picture is distorted relative to the other.

Another big difference between the world map and the deepest hole diagram is that Euclid's postulates include a compass in addition to a straightedge.  The First Law is the spacetime analog of a straightedge for spacetime, but at least in Newtonian spacetime, there is no analogue for a compass.  Straight lines are meaningful in spacetime, but circles are not.  (In the case of very high speed motion, a circle analogue does appear, but it turns out to be a hyperbola - a very different shape.  So while high speed "Minkowski" spacetime geometry is more like Euclidean geometry than Newton's low speed spacetime, it still has quite a different flavor.)  The existence of both compass and straightedge is what makes it obvious that uninterrupted world maps are distorted - the distortion is not arguable as it is in the case of Newtonian spacetime.


Local formulation of inverse square law
---------------------------------------

For Newton's global picture of gravity as a force, the inverse square law provides a natural description of mass as the source of gravity: Each particle of mass produces a attractive force on all other masses that varies inversely as the square of their separation.  However, in this form at least, the inverse square law is not a very natural description of the source of spacetime curvature for Einstein's local picture.  Instead, a century before Einstein, Gauss invented a local formulation of the inverse square law which does naturally describe the picture of gravity as spacetime curvature.  Gauss's local version also somewhat relaxes the action-at-a-distance feature of the inverse square law, which greatly bothered Newton himself.

The motion of a single planet is restricted to a plane, and in Newton's derivation, the inverse square ultimately comes from the reciprocal of the area (a distance squared) being swept out in that plane.  However, it is clear that Earth's gravitational attraction extends outward in three dimensions.  Its magnitude is constant on spheres centered on its source, like the surface of the Earth.  Since the area of a sphere varies directly as the square of its radius, it is tempting to think of the inverse square relation as the inverse of the area of a sphere enclosing the source.  That picture is the germ of Gauss's local formulation of the inverse square law.

### How to picture a vector field

Newton's acceleration of gravity is a vector; it has direction as well as magnitude, and we usually picture it as an arrow with length proportional to magnitude pointing in the direction of the acceleration.  However, in this case, we have to imagine not just a single vector, but a vector at every point in space - called a vector field - since the acceleration of gravity varies from place to place.  While there is nothing wrong with picturing a vector field as an enormous collection of little arrows, there is an equivalent alternative which sometimes can be more helpful:

Picture a vector field as fur - a collection of individual hairs filling 3D space lined up in a single direction in every small region, but slowly changing direction as you move from place to place.  You have to imagine you can distinguish the direction from root to tip of each hair - imagine each hair has a forward and backward.  (You can't stroke a cat's fur backward, after all.)  So much for the direction of the vectors, but what about their magnitude?  Take the magnitude of the vector field in any small region to be proportional to the density of the fur, or quantitatively to the number of hairs per unit area.  If the magnitude of the vectors is decreasing, it means the fur is fluffing out; if increasing, the fur is being more tightly packed together.

But what, exactly, does "number of hairs per unit area" mean?  That count depends on which area you choose, in particular on the orientation of that area.  We choose small planar loops - small compared to the distance over which the direction or density of fur changes, so the number of hairs threading the loop will be proportional to the area of the loop, since an adjacent second loop of the same size and orientation will be threaded by the same number of hairs.  As far as orientation, the number of hairs threading the loop will vary from zero whenever the plane of the loop contains any of the hairs, to a maximum when the plane of the loop is perpendicular to the local hair direction.  Note that you should also consider the loop to have a front and a back side, and count each hair as +1 if it threads from back to front, but -1 if it threads from front to back.

Comparing this variation in loop orientation to the arrow picture of a vector, you see that the variation of number of hairs per unit area with loop orientation has a very simple interpretation: The number of hairs per unit area threading the loop is (proportional to) the *component* of the vector in the direction normal to the loop.  In fact, the variation of hair count with loop orientation is arguably an easier way to understand vector components than imagining little right triangles with vector arrows for sides.

Vector addition is also very easy to understand in this fur picture of vector fields, as long as you allow the fur associated with a second source to pass through the fur from the first source without any interference (it is imaginary fur, after all).  You just add the hairs threading the loop from both sources (with due allowance for the direction they threaded) to find the total net number of hairs and hence the magnitude of the component of the sum vector normal to the loop.

What does the fur analogue for the inverse square law acceleration vector look like?  Consider two spheres concentric with the center of attraction.  The fur must everywhere point radially inward.  Since the total surface area of each sphere is directly proportional to the square of its radius, and the inverse square law demands that the number of hairs crossing inward per unit area vary inversely as the square of the radius (distance from center), the total number of hairs crossing the two spheres must be equal.  That is, the inverse square law means that you can regard each hair as a continuous strand running from infinity all the way to the center of attraction - no hairs end at any point other than the center, or begin at any finite point.  The inverse square law is a single head of hair standing perfectly on end, perhaps someone with very long hair connected to a van de Graaff generator.

### Counting hairs

Now Newton's idea for universal gravitation is that to determine the gravitational acceleration due to any collection of bodies, you break down large bodies into many small parts, then add up (as vectors) the accelerations due to each individual small part.  This principle, now called superposition, means that once you know the acceleration field for a tiny mass, you can simply add up the acceleration fields from all those tiny masses to get the acceleration field for to the whole collection.

If you think about the accelerations as tiny arrows and try to add them together, you soon get buried in a lot of complicated math.  Newton, being Newton, just pushed through that complicated math for the case of a planet-sized sphere composed of innumerable tiny chunks of mass to show that the acceleration of gravity we feel on Earth's surface is the same as if the entire mass of the Earth were located at its center.  But given the simplicity of this answer, he was annoyed that he couldn't find a simple geometrical way to add up all those vectors.

A century later, Gauss supplied that simpler picture, by thinking of the vector field more like our fur model, where instead of little vector arrows to indicate magnitude and direction, you think of the direction of the hairs with magnitude proportional to the density of the fur.  You know that the fur for each individual tiny mass is simply a bunch of radial hairs starting at infinity in every direction, and ending at the point mass.  Because of Newton's superposition principle, the hairs converging on any one tiny mass simply pass through those converging on any other.  If we want to find the component of the acceleration at any point and in any given direction, we just count the total number of hairs (terminating at every tiny mass) per unit area crossing through a little loop whose plane is normal to the acceleration component we want.  No need to add little arrows pointing in different directions - the orientation of the loop is common to all the hairs we're counting.

At first, it seems counting all the hairs from the swarm of tiny masses isn't any easier than adding up those little vector arrows.  But Gauss noticed that something delightful happens when you count all the hairs passing through not an infinitesimal loop, but instead through a finite closed surface.  In that case, it turns out to be easy to count the hairs, no matter what the size and shape of the surface!  Consider only the hairs attached to a single one ot the tiny masses.  If the mass is outside the closed surface, you know that any hair terminating on it cannot have stopped inside, so that hair will have exited the surface as many times as it entered (both may be zero) and cannot contribute to the overall count of hairs crossing into the surface as a whole.  Similarly, if the mass is inside the closed surface, the net number of times any hair terminating on it has crossed from outside to inside must be exactly one, since the hair began outside at infinity and wound up inside.  Thus, the total number of hairs crossing into any closed surface is just the total number of hairs converging on all the tiny masses inside that surface.

Note that the density of hairs representing any particular value of acceleration is something we have to choose arbitrarily.  We need to be sure that density is high enough that there are always a large number of hairs passing through whatever small loops we are using.  If you find yourself in a situation where the hair density is low enough that you have to worry about the spaces between individual hairs, you can imagine splitting each individual strand into a large number of parallel thinner hairs, and allowing those sub-hairs to expand to a uniform density cloud filling all the gaps between the original hairs.  In other words, there is no reason you have to be restricted an integer number of hairs to get a density - if necessary, you can count down to whatever fraction of a hair you please.

In the case of gravitational acceleration, each hair terminates on some small mass, along with many others converging from all directions.  For definiteness, suppose a thousand hairs converge on each gram of mass.  Then each individual hair represents a milligram of mass, to be subdivided further as required to be sure that some suitably large number thread through whatever small loop you happen to be considering.  From Gauss's counting method, you see that each hair in our fur model of gravitational acceleration represents a certain amount of mass - this hair's share of the mass that will be found at its end.

Mathematically, Gauss's counting method is one in a whole family of higher dimensional versions of the fundamental theorem of calculus - how to reconstruct a finite result from an infinite number of infinitesimal pieces.  Armed with Gauss's method, you can make short work of the special case Newton labored over - adding the accelerations toward every individual rock in the Earth to get the total acceleration of gravity we feel at the surface: Just take the closed surface to be the surface of the Earth. 

### Gauss's law

Instead, let's carve the Earth into tiny cubes in order to discover the differential, or local, form of the inverse square law.  The net number of hairs entering any small surface is proportional to the mass inside - a milligram per hair in our example.  Imagine cubes small enough that the material each one encloses is uniform.  In particular, the mass inside will be the material density times its volume.  Thus, the number of hairs stopping inside each little cube (not just passing through) per unit volume will equal the mass density of the material inside.  That ratio - number of hairs stopping inside a tiny cube per unit volume - depends only on the local geometry of the acceleration field, so you have found a local geometrical property of the field which is equal to mass density, a local physical property of the material.

If the acceleration of gravity were the same at every place, then the hairs would all be parallel and their density would be unchanging.  No hairs would stop inside our tiny cube or any other closed shape.  Hence, only if the acceleration changes from place to place can there be any net number of hairs entering a tiny cube.  In fact, it is not difficult to convince yourself that the net number of hairs entering any small cube must be proportional to the gradient of the gravitational acceleration across the cube: Only differences in acceleration from one side of the cube to the opposite mean that some must have stopped inside, and if those differences were to double, the number which stopped inside would also be double.  But acceleration is a vector with three components, any one of which may change differently in any of three directions, so the gradient of acceleration is rather a complicated thing with nine components.  The ratio of the number of hairs entering the cube to its volume, on the other hand, is just a single number for any one cube, so it is only one component or combination of components out of nine of the full acceleration gradient.

That ratio, in the limit of an infinitesimal cube, is called the *divergence* of the acceleration, and it is an important part of the gradient of acceleration.  In general, divergence measures the local accumulation of the hairs representing any vector field within small volumes.  Actually, as you might guess from the name, which ought to be "convergence" from this description, there is a minus sign for the case of the gravitational acceleration field.  You have now demonstrated Gauss's Law for the gravitational acceleration: The divergence of the acceleration at any position is proportional to (minus) the mass density of the material there.

Again, divergence is one component of the full field gradient.  Recall that the compound curvature of spacetime in our deepest hole example caused gaps to open in our spacetime diagram, analogous to the interruptions in an undistorted world map.  The size of these gaps was also proportional to the gradient of the gravitational acceleration.  Now the full spacetime curvature at any given point (Riemann curvature in math jargon) has many different components, all the non-zero ones being the full gradient of the gravitational acceleration field.  Gauss's law thus identifies one particular component of the full curvature at every point in space - the divergence of the acceleration of gravity - with (minus) the mass density at that point!

Gauss's law is the local formulation of Newton's inverse square law which fits naturally into Einstein's picture of gravity as local spacetime curvature.  Just as mass is the source of gravity in Newton's global picture, mass *density* causes spacetime to be curved in Einstein's local picture.  In fact, Gauss's law is the single non-zero component of Einstein's field equations of general relativity in the Newtonian low speed limit.

The information content of Gauss's Law is a considerable part of Newton's inverse square law of universal gravitation.  However, it is not quite equivalent to Newton's law.  Mathematically, knowing the divergence of the acceleration is not enough to determine the acceleration, as knowing the distribution of mass would - remember that divergence is only one piece of the gradient.  One other piece, called the curl, measures the extent to which the vectors twist around into vortices.  The curl of any central force, like gravity, is always zero.  Together, knowing the local values of the divergence (proportional to density for gravity) and curl (zero for gravity) of any vector field at every point does uniquely determine the field, with one caveat - you need to know that the value of the field at infinity is zero.  This caveat is shared with Newton's inverse square form of the law - you need to know that you've gone out far enough to include all of the masses which could have any significant contribution to the acceleration of gravity.

The absence of the condition that the curl of the gravitational acceleration must be zero from Einstein's field equations is worth mentioning.  In Newtonian gravity, we haven't discussed the gravitational potential associated with the acceleration of gravity.  Any central force is the gradient of a potential function, which is just a single number at every point.  The three components of the acceleration come from the direction of the gradient at each point.  Now the curl of any vector field which is itself a gradient is automatically zero.  Implicit in Einstein's field equations is the assumption that spacetime has a metric - an analogue of distance in ordinary space.  (Newtonian spacetime has no metric, or rather has two separate metrics for time and space.)  The connection coefficients - including the acceleration of gravity - can be derived from the gradient of this metric, in a manner analogous to the way acceleration is derived from a potential in Newtonian mechanics.  Just as the curl of acceleration is automatically zero because it is derived from a potential, the analogue of the curl of the connection coefficients is automatically zero because they are derived from a metric.  The potential in Newtonian gravity plays the role of the metric in Einstein's full theory.
